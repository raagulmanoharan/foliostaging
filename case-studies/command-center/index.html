Command Center

Connecting signals. Understanding risk. Acting sooner.

A shared operational workspace for Salesforce Industries. I led the runtime design and helped teams explore and adopt the framework.

Built once. Used across industries.

Salesforce’s industry teams build specialized solutions on the core platform. My team, Common Services, builds the tools and frameworks they share.

Life Sciences needed a command center. Regional leaders were tracking signals across systems and wanted to spot performance risks before they affected quarterly goals. Understanding a trend, investigating its causes and coordinating a response happened in different places.

We brought Life Sciences in as a design partner and framed the opportunity more broadly: a reusable operational workspace that other industry teams could configure for their own users.

From first glance to next action.

The landing experience, alerts and watchlist form one journey. Design-time tools let industry teams define what that experience monitors.

01Balance noise with insight.

The early console gave metrics and alerts similar weight. It showed the data, but left the person to rank it. When I played an operational day forward in the simulator, the volume of alerts made that problem harder to ignore: a realistic day produced more interruptions than someone could review.

Digest

What changed while I was away?

A short briefing
Alerts

What needs my attention?

Prioritized intervention
Watchlist

What am I keeping an eye on?

Ongoing monitoring

I separated three jobs. The digest explains what changed; ranked alerts identify where intervention may be needed; the watchlist holds the metrics a person chooses to follow. Home shows a limited set of alerts with a visible total and a route to the full list, rather than quietly hiding the rest.

This trades breadth on the landing page for a clearer order of attention. The underlying detail remains available when someone chooses to investigate.

02Design the path between screens.

The first console put alerts in a tray beside the work. Closing the tray also removed the place where someone could return to the issue. I gave alerts a persistent destination and kept broader conversations as saved threads, so an investigation could continue beyond the session in which it started.

The platform imposed another boundary: Salesforce already owned the global header, launcher and search. Our navigation had to fit within one rail. Rather than absorb existing industry applications, Command Center links into them when the work needs to go deeper.

The resulting structure gives each job a place: Home to orient, All Alerts to take responsibility, Watchlist to keep monitoring and Quick Chats to return to a question. A metric uses the same card pattern on the watchlist, in an answer and when pinned beside a conversation.

03Make the answer something people can check.

In the conversation-only approach, the same alert could be explained in a different order to different people. That is a problem when someone needs to share an issue, justify a decision or return to the original reasoning.

I gave the alert a stable evidence structure: explanation, contributing factors, sources, supporting chart and recommended actions. The companion extends that record rather than becoming the only place the explanation exists. The design also separates generated interpretation from system data.

Engineering’s approach of generating the analysis when the alert is created supports a consistent record, rather than regenerating it for every reader. We chose human approval for changes to data. That gives up speed on routine fixes, but preserves the ability to review and override a proposed action.

04Understand the request before replacing the interface.

Customers described the dashboard as dated and click-heavy, and asked for a more conversational experience. The initial product direction was to replace it. I questioned whether that request meant people wanted to give up seeing important signals immediately.

Dashboard

Immediate visibility.
More work to interpret.

Conversation-first

Flexible exploration.
Requires a starting question.

Hybrid

Visible signals.
Contextual exploration.

Working with research, we made the alternatives concrete in running prototypes. The dashboard exposed information overload and the missing “why.” Conversation-first created a different burden: people had to know what to ask before seeing the situation. The evaluation supported keeping visibility and flexibility together.

The hybrid direction separates tasks rather than simply putting chat next to charts. A known alert opens as a record with a contextual companion. A broad question opens into a larger conversation where charts have room. The starting card stays in view so follow-ups remain anchored to the same subject.

05Design the setup behind the experience.

A reusable runtime is only half the system. The same framework must let an industry team define what matters in its domain without designing a new alert experience from scratch.

The framework keeps layouts and shared interaction patterns consistent while allowing domain-specific alert definitions and thresholds. That boundary matters: the builder configures the meaning of the alert; the operational user gets a familiar way to read, investigate and respond to it.

The actual alert-setup screens are still needed to show this decision in detail. The reserved space below will hold the configuration flow and the preview of its runtime result.

06Extend the framework beyond the workspace.

Headless setup for forward-deployed engineers belongs here as a separate design moment.

Give teams something they can run.

The framework had to work for the teams adopting it, too. I built a sandbox and playground with sample industry data and a simulator so partners could experience changing conditions, try their own scenarios and discuss the same working artifact.

Shared patterns, setup guidance and extension rules gave teams a starting point they could build on. The playground made both the experience and its constraints easier to evaluate together.

From a shared direction to a pilot.

Dreamforce

The Command Center experience was presented at Dreamforce. The prototype made the shared direction tangible; it should not be confused with general availability.

Pilot

The team took the hybrid experience to pilot. Industry teams also used the playground to develop their own designs, carrying the shared framework beyond the core prototype.

Pilot results are not available for this case study. Adoption, operational improvements and business impact are not claimed here.

Make the problem—and the trade-offs—clear.

The work connected two needs: helping operational users make informed decisions, and giving industry teams a framework they could reuse.

My role was to bring questions and a point of view early, use artifacts to make alternatives concrete, and refine decisions with research, product and engineering. The system became clearer when we designed the relationships between its parts, not just the screens.
