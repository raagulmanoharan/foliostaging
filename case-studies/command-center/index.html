Project context

The problem lives between the dashboards.

People responsible for operations need to know what needs attention, why it matters and who can respond. The signals behind a problem often sit in different systems, owned by different teams. Seeing a metric change is only the beginning of the work.

Command Center brings those signals into a shared operational workspace. It helps people get their bearings, investigate a risk and review a response—without rebuilding the context at each step.

I led design for the runtime experience: the home, alerts, investigation flows and shared interaction patterns. The challenge was also organizational. Different Salesforce industry teams needed to extend the product for their own users while preserving one understandable experience.

The design challenge

How do we turn scattered signals into a decision someone can understand and act on?

Final artifact

From a signal to a useful next step.

The experience follows one operational journey. A leader gets an overview, notices a risk, checks the evidence and explores possible responses. The screens below have reserved places for the actual product captures supplied in the image manifest.

01 / Orient

A clear place to start.

A briefing, prioritized alerts and a personal watchlist bring relevant changes into view. The composer gives people a way to pursue a question of their own.

02 / Understand

Keep the evidence with the alert.

A consistent alert detail brings together the explanation, contributing factors and supporting evidence. People can inspect what prompted the warning before deciding what to do.

03 / Evaluate

Explore a response before committing.

Conversation extends the investigation. Charts and cards make answers inspectable; proposed actions remain subject to human review. Broader questions have space outside the alert-specific flow.

Key design moments

Make the trade-offs tangible.

01

Design the path between the screens.

I broke the experience down into the user’s goal at each step: notice something meaningful, understand it, investigate uncertainty and decide how to respond. This gave the team a starting point for discussing scope and trade-offs beyond individual screens.

Detection and investigation needed different levels of detail. Home could orient someone without becoming the entire workspace. The alert could hold a dependable record, while the companion supported questions grounded in that record.

02

Question the meaning behind the request.

Customers described an early dashboard as dated and asked for a more conversational experience. The initial product direction was to replace the dashboard. I questioned whether the request meant people wanted to lose its immediate visibility.

Working with research, we explored the alternatives with customers. They valued conversational flexibility, but still needed important signals in front of them—including the ones they would not know to ask about.

The clearer requirement was to make important information visible by default and give people flexibility in how they explore it. Prototypes made that trade-off concrete enough for design, product and engineering to evaluate together.

03

Make the answer something people can check.

An explanation needs more than a confident response. The alert experience brings the cause, contributing factors and evidence into a consistent structure. A person can inspect the basis of a recommendation before acting.

I connected alert-specific investigation and open exploration through shared card and composer patterns. The scope changes, while familiar interaction rules help people stay oriented. Proposals remain distinct from changes a person has approved.

04

Give teams a system they can work with.

A static frame could not show an operational day unfolding. I built a playground with sample industry data and simulation so teams could experience signals arriving, alerts competing for attention and investigations developing over time.

Playing through those scenarios exposed alert noise that static reviews had not surfaced. It made prioritization and changing context tangible, giving cross-functional discussions a common artifact rather than an imagined experience.

The playground also gave industry teams a shared starting point. Reusable patterns, setup guidance and rules for extending cards and alerts helped teams explore their own use cases within a coherent framework.

Impact

A product direction—and a foundation to build on.

The team took the hybrid approach to pilot. Research helped clarify the relationship between visual monitoring and conversational investigation, giving the team a stronger basis for evaluating the product direction.

Industry teams used the playground to develop their own designs. The reusable patterns and guidance extended the work beyond a single prototype and supported a shared approach to Command Center.

Pilot results are not available for this case study. Adoption, operational response time and business performance remain to be measured.

Reflection

Design the relationships, not only the surfaces.

My contribution was to bring user understanding and design judgment into decisions alongside research, business priorities and platform constraints. Questions and prototypes helped us make assumptions visible, discuss alternatives and refine the direction together.

The most useful artifacts connected the parts of the system: a signal to its evidence, an investigation to a proposed action, and a shared framework to the teams extending it.

Next time, I would establish pilot measures earlier—particularly whether people can recognize a meaningful signal, understand its evidence and reach a useful next step. A preferred prototype is a stronger direction to test, not proof of better operational outcomes.
